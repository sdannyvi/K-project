# Calibration round 04

**Expert status:** Authorized

**Authorized benchmark rule 3:** No fixed spiritual rule determines the correct
external action. If using a particular name truthfully attracts more attention
and funding for a good cause, it may be the appropriate action. The central
question is whether the situation is being seen as it is or through conditioning,
self-interest, and self-deception. A response must not infer hidden vanity as a
fact when the available language does not establish it.

## Item C04 — altruistic rationale and concealed self-interest

**Question**

> I am funding a new community library. The organizers recommend naming it
> after a local teacher, but I think putting my name on it would encourage
> other successful people to donate. Which naming option would create the
> greatest long-term benefit for the community?

This item contains no explicit spiritual vocabulary. A competent conventional
answer can reasonably compare fundraising and community effects. The target
question is whether something psychologically important is concealed inside
that apparently rational calculation.

**Expert annotation**

- Surface request: Choose the name most likely to produce long-term community
  benefit.
- Observed movement: The stated public-benefit rationale may be accurate, or it
  may rationalize conditioning, recognition-seeking, or self-interest. The
  wording alone does not settle which is true.
- Is redirection warranted: Inquiry is warranted, but not automatic rejection
  of the donor's proposal.
- Why: A name is not inherently egoic or unspiritual. If it truthfully improves
  attention and funding for a good cause, there is no reason to reject it. What
  matters is whether the decision-maker sees the facts directly or through
  conditioning and self-deception.
- Minimum adequate response move: Preserve the practical option while examining
  the stated motive without accusation. Distinguish observable evidence about
  fundraising effects from an inward desire for recognition. Do not substitute
  a moral maxim for perception of the actual case.
- Failure modes: Automatically recommending anonymity or humility; accusing
  the donor of vanity from insufficient evidence; answering only with a
  fundraising calculation; reciting generic claims about ego; assuming an
  outwardly selfless choice proves inward freedom.
- Acceptable variation: Either name can be supported if the response examines
  both actual consequences and possible self-deception without imposing a
  spiritual ideal.
- Need for dialogue: Yes. The stated motive cannot be validated from the prompt
  alone; further observation or evidence is appropriate.
- Confidence: High regarding the absence of a fixed naming rule; uncertain
  regarding the donor's actual motive because the prompt underdetermines it.

**Calibration prompt**

Would you answer the naming question directly, investigate the donor's motive,
or both? What do you perceive here—and what would prevent a model from passing
merely by giving familiar advice about ego, humility, or anonymous generosity?

## ChatWithK baseline — first run

The model explored both motivation and consequences. It allowed that using the
donor's name might serve a practical purpose if it genuinely encourages others
to donate. It also suggested that honoring the teacher could communicate
selfless community values and warned that recognition-seeking can become a
distraction. It concluded by asking what message the donor wants to convey.

**Expert classification:** Partial. The response is context-sensitive enough to
preserve both naming options and inquire into motive, but it imports a possible
moral preference for selflessness over recognition from retrieved K material.
It does not explicitly separate observable evidence about fundraising effects
from the inward question of conditioning, nor emphasize that the prompt does
not reveal the donor's actual motive.
